# Severe Coronary Artery Calcification in a 67-Year-Old Man

## Purpose and boundaries

This report is a clinician-grade evidence map and Australian care-navigation guide for a 67-year-old man whose August–September 2026 cardiac imaging reportedly showed significant coronary artery calcification (CAC). It is not a diagnosis, does not replace a treating GP or cardiologist, and cannot interpret the individual case without the original reports, symptoms, medication list, examination, ECG, blood pressure, lipid profile, kidney function and relevant history.

No finite document can contain “everything known in the history of mankind.” This corpus instead captures the durable science, the major clinical controversies, the key management pathways, and the practical public/private Australian context most likely to affect decisions now.

## Immediate safety rule

**Call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance now** if there is severe, worsening or persistent chest pressure/pain—especially beyond 10 minutes—or breathlessness, sweating, nausea, faintness, palpitations, or discomfort spreading to the arm, shoulder, jaw, neck or back. Do not drive yourself. Australian authorities treat a possible heart attack as an emergency because only urgent clinical assessment, ECG and biomarkers can distinguish it safely.[^1][^2][^3]

A calcium score is a long-term risk marker, not a test that can rule an acute coronary syndrome in or out. The 2025 Australian acute coronary syndrome guideline specifically says not to rely on CAC scoring alone when evaluating ACS.[^4]

## Bottom-line interpretation

“Significant coronary calcification” means there is definite coronary atherosclerosis and a substantial accumulated plaque burden. It **does not by itself establish** which artery is narrowed, the percentage stenosis, whether blood flow is impaired, whether a particular plaque is about to rupture, or whether a stent or bypass is required.[^5][^6][^7]

For an asymptomatic 67-year-old, the usual priority is rapid but orderly clinical review and intensive prevention—not panic and not automatic invasive angiography. Symptoms, the exact Agatston score and percentile, coronary distribution, any CT-coronary-angiography stenoses, left ventricular function, ECG, diabetes, kidney disease, smoking, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, family history and current treatment determine what should happen next.

If the finding was from a non-contrast calcium scan, the scan measured plaque burden but not the arterial lumen. If it was from CT coronary angiography (CTCA), the report may also describe non-calcified plaque, stenosis, high-risk anatomy and image limitations. These are materially different reports and should not be treated as interchangeable.[^8][^9][^5]

## The essential distinction

| Question | CAC scan | CT coronary angiography | Functional stress imaging | Invasive angiography |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main question | How much calcified coronary plaque exists? | Is plaque present and does it narrow the lumen? | Does exertion or pharmacological stress produce myocardial ischaemia? | What is the lumen anatomy, and can physiology/intervention be performed? |
| Contrast | No | Iodinated contrast | Depends on modality | Iodinated contrast |
| Detects non-calcified plaque | No | Yes | No direct plaque imaging | Lumen silhouette, not full plaque composition |
| Measures stenosis | No | Yes, although heavy calcium can impair accuracy | Indirectly assesses functional significance | Yes; intermediate lesions should generally undergo FFR/iFR when relevant |
| Best role | Risk refinement in selected asymptomatic people | First-line anatomical test in many symptomatic patients | Correlating symptoms with ischaemia and quantifying burden | Very high likelihood, high-risk findings, refractory symptoms or planned intervention |
| Core limitation | Cannot identify flow limitation or vulnerable non-calcified plaque | Radiation, contrast, heart-rate/image-quality issues and calcium “blooming” | False positives/negatives; does not show full anatomy | Invasive bleeding, vascular, kidney, stroke and procedural risks |

Current chronic coronary syndrome guidance favours non-invasive anatomical or functional imaging first, with CTCA preferred to rule out obstructive disease and identify non-obstructive plaque, functional imaging preferred to relate symptoms to ischaemia, and invasive angiography reserved for high-risk or unresolved situations.[^9][^10]

## What calcium represents

Coronary calcification is not passive “limescale.” It is an active, regulated component of atherosclerosis involving inflammation, cell death, extracellular vesicles, vascular smooth-muscle-cell phenotypic change and deposition of calcium-phosphate hydroxyapatite in the arterial intima.[^11][^12][^13]

Atherosclerotic calcification evolves from microscopic deposits into punctate, fragmented, sheet-like or nodular calcium. Tiny microcalcifications may contribute to local plaque stress, while large, dense calcium often reflects older, more stable fibrocalcific plaque; nevertheless, a large total calcium burden signals extensive lifetime coronary atherosclerosis and higher patient-level event risk.[^14][^7][^13]

Intimal coronary calcification should be distinguished from medial Mönckeberg calcification, which is more characteristic of peripheral vessels, diabetes, chronic kidney disease and disordered mineral metabolism. In coronary arteries, CT-detected calcium is overwhelmingly used as a marker of atherosclerotic plaque burden.[^14][^11]

## How scoring developed

Arthur Agatston and colleagues introduced the practical CT calcium score in 1990 using ultrafast electron-beam CT. The Agatston method sums coronary lesions based on calcified area and a density weighting, traditionally identifying foci at or above 130 Hounsfield units; modern studies generally use ECG-gated multidetector CT.[^15][^16]

The score is reported in Agatston units (AU), often alongside an age/sex/ethnicity percentile. The absolute score estimates total burden; the percentile indicates how unusual that burden is among peers. Australian guidance treats a score above 99 AU or at least the 75th percentile as capable of reclassifying selected primary-prevention patients to high absolute risk.[^17]

## Interpreting the number

| Agatston score | Conventional description | Practical meaning |
|---:|---|---|
| 0 | No CT-detectable calcium | Very low near-term risk in many asymptomatic adults, but not proof of no plaque or no stenosis |
| 1–99/100 | Mild | Definite atherosclerosis; treatment depends on total risk and risk enhancers |
| 100–400 | Moderate | Substantial plaque burden; Australian sources generally support high-risk reclassification above 99 AU |
| Greater than 400 | Severe/extensive | High event risk and increased likelihood of obstructive disease; requires intensive prevention and symptom-led assessment |
| At least 1,000 | Extreme | Very high-risk phenotype; observational cohorts show risks approaching some treated secondary-prevention populations |

Australian RACGP material uses 0, 1–100, 100–400 and greater than 400 as very-low, low, moderate and high categories; thresholds are guides rather than biological cliffs. In the CAC Consortium, asymptomatic people with scores at least 1,000 had roughly twice the mortality rate of those with scores 400–999, with cardiovascular mortality rates of 8.0 versus 3.6 per 1,000 person-years. Separate analyses suggest very high CAC around 900–1,000 can carry event rates similar to stable, treated secondary-prevention trial populations, although this does not make every such patient clinically identical to someone who has already had a myocardial infarction.[^18][^19][^20][^5]

The exact report matters more than a vague adjective. A useful extraction includes:

- Total Agatston score and percentile.
- Vessel-level scores for left main, LAD, circumflex and right coronary arteries.
- Whether disease is focal or diffuse and whether the left main or proximal LAD is involved.
- Whether this was a dedicated CAC scan, incidental calcium on a routine chest CT, or CTCA.
- On CTCA: maximal stenosis by vessel, CAD-RADS category, plaque composition, high-risk plaque features, image quality and any non-diagnostic segments.
- Cardiac structure and function from echocardiography, if performed.

## What the score cannot tell you

The relationship between CAC and narrowing is probabilistic, not one-to-one. Arteries may enlarge outward during plaque development, so a large plaque burden can coexist with a relatively preserved lumen; conversely, non-calcified plaque can cause important stenosis even when CAC is zero.[^21][^6][^22]

CAC also does not identify a single “culprit” plaque. Acute coronary syndromes often arise when a lipid-rich or eroded plaque triggers thrombosis, and the lesion causing a future event may not be the most calcified or most narrowed lesion today. Calcification is therefore a patient-level burden/risk marker, not a countdown clock.

A score does not directly determine treatment benefit from PCI or bypass. Revascularisation decisions depend on symptoms, anatomy, physiological significance, left ventricular function and high-risk territories—not the calcium number alone.[^10][^9]

## Prognosis and uncertainty

Risk rises continuously with total CAC; categories simplify that continuum. In observational cohorts, CAC predicts coronary, cardiovascular and all-cause outcomes beyond conventional risk factors, but an individual’s absolute risk can be materially altered by age, diabetes, kidney disease, smoking, blood pressure, LDL, fitness, medications and the presence or absence of obstructive disease.[^23][^24]

At age 67, an elevated absolute score is less unusual than in a 40-year-old, but it is not benign. Both the absolute burden and percentile should be considered: absolute burden is strongly linked to near-term event risk, while a high percentile flags unusually advanced disease relative to peers.[^25][^17]

Extreme CAC is clinically important even in a symptom-free person, but evidence remains stronger for CAC as a risk-stratification tool than for routine downstream stress testing or angiography in every asymptomatic patient. Australian primary-care guidance explicitly discourages population screening, use in people already known to be high risk, and use as the investigation for chest pain.[^18][^5]

## The statin–calcium paradox

Serial CAC is a poor way to judge whether lipid treatment is working. Statins reduce lipid-rich and fibro-fatty plaque, slow overall plaque progression and promote denser calcification; because density is upweighted in the Agatston formula, the score may continue to rise despite lower cardiovascular risk.[^26][^27][^28]

This means:

- A rising CAC score after starting a statin does not automatically mean treatment failure.
- Calcium does not generally “melt away” when risk falls.
- Treatment response is better judged by adherence, LDL/non-HDL cholesterol or apoB response, blood pressure, smoking status, glycaemia, symptoms, function and clinical events.
- Repeat CAC is generally not useful when the result will not change management; Australian advice says not to repeat merely to monitor prevention.[^29][^5]

## Causes and risk amplifiers

CAC reflects cumulative exposure to causal and modifying factors rather than excess dietary calcium alone. Key drivers and enhancers include:

- Age and male sex.
- Elevated LDL-containing apoB particles, including lifelong exposure and familial hypercholesterolaemia.
- Hypertension.
- Smoking and second-hand smoke.
- Diabetes, insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome.
- Chronic kidney disease and albuminuria, especially with mineral-bone disorder.
- Family history of premature atherosclerotic disease.
- Elevated lipoprotein(a), an inherited apoB particle associated with atherosclerotic and calcific disease.
- Inflammatory disorders, sedentary behaviour, central adiposity, poor diet, sleep apnoea and psychosocial stress.

The clinically useful question is not “why did calcium appear?” but “which modifiable exposures remain, and what is the patient’s residual risk?” A broad, one-time baseline assessment is more valuable than unvalidated supplement panels.

## Recommended clinical dataset

A GP or cardiologist should reconcile the following before deciding on tests or treatment:

| Domain | Information to obtain |
|---|---|
| Symptoms | Exertional chest pressure, dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance, arm/jaw discomfort, rest symptoms, duration, progression and triggers |
| History | MI, stroke/TIA, peripheral arterial disease, diabetes, CKD, hypertension, sleep apnoea, inflammatory disease and bleeding history |
| Family | Premature MI/stroke, sudden death and known familial hypercholesterolaemia |
| Exposures | Current/former smoking, alcohol, diet, physical activity, weight trajectory and relevant drugs/supplements |
| Examination | Repeated seated BP, orthostatic BP when relevant, pulse/rhythm, BMI/waist, murmurs, heart failure and peripheral vascular signs |
| Basic tests | ECG; fasting or non-fasting lipids; HbA1c/glucose; creatinine/eGFR; urine ACR; liver enzymes; full blood count; TSH where indicated |
| Lipid refinement | Non-HDL-C, apoB where helpful, and a one-time Lp(a), especially with premature/familial or unexpectedly severe disease |
| Imaging | Original images/reports, CAC total and percentile, vessel distribution, CTCA stenoses/CAD-RADS, echocardiographic LV function and valve findings |
| Medicines | Exact doses, adherence, adverse effects, interactions, antithrombotics and NSAID use |

The Australian CVD calculator is designed for people without known CVD aged 45–79 and classifies five-year risk as low below 5%, intermediate 5% to below 10%, or high at least 10%. Once substantial imaging-defined coronary atherosclerosis is known, the calculator remains contextual but should not be allowed to downplay the direct evidence of disease.[^30][^31]

## A sensible next-step pathway

### If symptoms are acute or unstable

Use emergency assessment, not an outpatient calcium pathway. The Australian 2025 ACS guideline uses clinical assessment, serial ECG/troponin and risk-stratified anatomical or functional testing; for suitable intermediate-risk patients without known CAD, CTCA can be first line, but CAC alone is insufficient.[^4]

### If symptoms are stable

Prompt GP/cardiology review should classify pre-test likelihood and select the test that answers the clinical question. CTCA is useful for defining anatomy in many low-to-moderate-likelihood patients, while stress echocardiography, SPECT/PET or stress CMR is useful when the central question is inducible ischaemia or when severe calcium may limit CTCA interpretation.[^9][^10]

At age 67 with heavy CAC, test selection should account for local expertise, renal function, heart rhythm, ability to exercise and the likelihood that calcium blooming will make CTCA segments uninterpretable. An equivocal CTCA may lead to functional imaging; high-risk anatomy, extensive ischaemia or refractory angina may lead to invasive angiography with FFR/iFR where appropriate.[^32][^10]

### If entirely asymptomatic

First confirm the exact report, treat modifiable risk aggressively and assess for clinically silent clues such as reduced exercise tolerance, LV dysfunction or abnormal ECG. Routine stress testing or invasive angiography solely because CAC is high has not been shown to improve outcomes for all asymptomatic patients; selective investigation is reasonable when extreme burden, high-risk distribution, abnormal function or occupational/clinical circumstances materially change the question.[^33][^18]

## Treatment pillars

### Lipid lowering

Statins are first-line because event reduction tracks the absolute LDL reduction. Across large randomised trial meta-analyses, each 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C produced about a 21–22% proportional reduction in major vascular events over roughly five years.[^34][^35]

For an Australian primary-prevention patient, the 2023 guideline recommends lipid-modifying therapy at a calculated five-year risk of at least 10% and consideration at 5% to below 10%, with shared decision-making. Imaging-confirmed substantial plaque is a powerful reason for a clinician to favour intensive treatment even when untreated LDL appears “normal.”[^36][^37]

Practical escalation usually proceeds through:

- A high-potency statin at the highest tolerated dose, commonly atorvastatin or rosuvastatin.
- Add ezetimibe if the agreed LDL reduction/level is not achieved or if statin dose is limited.
- Consider a PCSK9-targeted drug for selected very-high-risk or familial-hypercholesterolaemia patients who remain above threshold despite optimised oral therapy, recognising strict PBS criteria.[^38][^39][^40]

Australia’s 2023 primary-prevention guideline deliberately does not impose one universal LDL target. Australian expert practice often borrows risk-based targets: less than 1.8 mmol/L is used by some public services for stable imaging-defined coronary disease, while established chronic coronary syndrome/very-high-risk international guidance uses less than 1.4 mmol/L plus at least a 50% reduction. The treating clinician should state explicitly whether the patient is being managed as high-risk primary prevention, imaging-defined chronic coronary disease, or secondary prevention.[^41][^42][^33]

Suspected “statin intolerance” should be evaluated systematically rather than abandoning LDL lowering: document timing and pattern, check interactions and reversible contributors, rechallenge with a different statin or schedule when safe, and combine lower-dose statin with non-statin therapy if needed.

### Blood pressure

Repeated, properly measured BP and home readings are more informative than one clinic value. Australian guidance recommends BP-lowering pharmacotherapy for high absolute risk and consideration for intermediate risk; BP above 160/100 mmHg warrants treatment regardless of the calculator.[^37][^30]

The exact target should be individualised for frailty, falls, kidney function and orthostatic symptoms. In established chronic coronary syndrome, contemporary European guidance aims for treated systolic BP around 120–129 mmHg when tolerated.[^42]

### Antiplatelet therapy

**Do not self-start aspirin.** At age 67 without prior MI, stroke, revascularisation or clinically established symptomatic ASCVD, this is primary prevention; bleeding risk often offsets cardiovascular benefit. The USPSTF recommends against initiating low-dose aspirin for primary prevention from age 60 and reports a 58% relative increase in major gastrointestinal bleeding across trials.[^43][^44]

Older Australian CAC statements suggested aspirin for CAC above 100, but contemporary primary-prevention evidence is more cautious. A clinician may still consider it in an exceptional very-high-ischaemic/low-bleeding-risk case, but only after reviewing ulcer or bleeding history, anticoagulants, NSAIDs, renal disease, blood pressure and patient preference. The equation changes after MI, PCI/CABG, stroke or another clear secondary-prevention indication.[^45][^46]

### Lifestyle as treatment

Lifestyle therapy complements rather than substitutes for indicated medication:

- Stop smoking completely; offer pharmacotherapy and behavioural support.
- Use a Mediterranean-style pattern rich in vegetables, legumes, fruit, whole grains, nuts, fish and unsaturated oils; reduce trans fat, saturated fat, refined carbohydrates and sodium.
- Accumulate regular moderate aerobic activity and resistance work, but obtain clinical guidance before suddenly beginning vigorous exercise if there are symptoms or very poor baseline fitness.
- Reduce sedentary time, preserve muscle and work toward a sustainable waist/weight trajectory.
- Treat diabetes, sleep apnoea and depression; optimise sleep and medication adherence.
- Avoid assuming calcium supplements caused coronary calcium; review supplements for need, dose and interactions rather than stopping prescribed treatment reflexively.

For established chronic coronary disease, guidance supports 30–60 minutes of moderate activity on more than five days per week and a Mediterranean dietary pattern, adjusted to individual safety and capacity.[^42]

### Anti-anginal treatment

If symptoms represent stable angina, beta blockers, calcium-channel blockers, nitrates and other agents may be used according to heart rate, BP, LV function, comorbidity and symptom pattern. Anti-anginal drugs improve symptoms; LDL, BP, smoking and antithrombotic decisions address event risk.

## Revascularisation: what it can and cannot do

A high calcium score is not an indication for a stent. In stable patients with moderate-to-severe ischaemia, ISCHEMIA found that an initial invasive strategy did not reduce all-cause death or the main composite outcome over the initial follow-up compared with optimal medical therapy, although invasive care improved angina-related quality of life most in those with frequent symptoms and changed the balance between procedural and spontaneous MI.[^47][^48][^49]

Revascularisation remains important for acute coronary syndromes, unacceptable angina despite medical therapy, selected high-risk anatomy such as significant left-main disease, and other anatomy/physiology where a heart team judges benefit. Severe calcification also makes PCI technically harder and may require intravascular imaging and plaque-modification technologies; that is a procedural planning issue, not a reason to intervene on an asymptomatic score.

## Monitoring

A practical follow-up plan generally includes:

- Review tolerance and adherence within weeks of treatment changes.
- Repeat lipids after a clinically appropriate interval to confirm percentage and absolute response.
- Monitor liver enzymes or creatine kinase when clinically indicated, not reflexively at every visit.
- Track home BP, HbA1c where relevant, renal function and smoking status.
- Reassess symptoms and functional capacity; new or worsening symptoms override a previously “stable” plan.
- Avoid serial CAC as the primary treatment scorecard. Repeat only if a future result would genuinely change a disputed management decision.[^50][^5]

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | More accurate statement |
|---|---|
| “Calcium means an artery is X% blocked.” | CAC measures calcified plaque burden, not lumen stenosis. |
| “A high score means a heart attack is imminent.” | It indicates elevated long-term risk, not timing. Acute symptoms determine urgency. |
| “A zero score means no coronary disease.” | Non-calcified plaque and even significant stenosis can occur with CAC zero, especially in symptomatic or younger people.[^21][^22] |
| “The highest-calcified plaque will rupture.” | CAC does not reliably identify the future culprit lesion. |
| “A rising score proves statins failed.” | Statins can densify/stabilise plaque while increasing the Agatston score.[^26][^27] |
| “Severe CAC means a stent is needed.” | PCI/CABG decisions require symptoms, anatomy and physiology. |
| “Aspirin is harmless insurance.” | In older primary-prevention adults, bleeding can outweigh benefit.[^43] |
| “Diet alone can reverse severe CAC.” | Diet is essential, but high plaque burden commonly warrants pharmacological risk reduction. |

## Australian healthcare pathway

### Primary care

A GP is the coordinating entry point. A comprehensive Heart Health Check/risk assessment can establish symptoms, BP, lipids, diabetes/renal status and treatment, while a referral enables Medicare rebates for private specialist care.[^51][^52]

The referral should attach the original imaging report/images where available, ECG, lipid profile, HbA1c, eGFR, urine ACR, full blood count, current medicines/allergies and a clear description of symptoms and urgency. Public services triage referrals according to clinical urgency and completeness.[^53][^33]

### CAC funding

As of June 2026, Australia still had no MBS item for dedicated CT-CAC; the patient generally self-funds it, while an application for an MBS item was under assessment. This aligns with Heart Foundation patient information stating CAC is not covered by Medicare.[^54][^55][^56]

Repeating a self-funded CAC scan soon after a severe result usually adds cost and radiation without changing treatment. The more valuable spend is often clinical review, risk-factor treatment and a test targeted to symptoms/anatomy when indicated.

### CTCA funding

CTCA is different from CAC and can receive an MBS rebate when criteria are met. In 2026, MBS item 57360 required a specialist/consultant-physician request plus stable or acute symptoms consistent with coronary ischaemia and low-to-intermediate acute-event risk; the schedule fee was $808.10, with listed 75% and 85% benefits of $606.10 and $703.60.[^57][^58]

MBS eligibility is not a guarantee of no gap: radiology providers may charge above the schedule fee, and a specialist consultation/referral may also involve a gap. Ask for the item number, total fee, Medicare rebate and estimated out-of-pocket cost in writing.

### Public route in Victoria

A Medicare-eligible person treated as a public patient in a public hospital does not pay for hospital treatment, but access is catchment- and triage-dependent and may involve waiting. Emergency symptoms should go through 000/ED; stable findings go through GP referral to the relevant public cardiology service.[^59][^51]

Not every Melbourne public clinic accepts isolated elevated CAC. Royal Melbourne Hospital explicitly says it cannot accept stable coronary disease—including elevated calcium score or incidental CT calcification—without active chest pain or breathlessness, and instead advises aggressive control of BP, cholesterol, diabetes, weight and smoking. Other networks use Victorian statewide criteria and local catchments, so the GP should direct the referral to the patient’s health service rather than assuming any tertiary centre will accept it.[^60][^61][^62][^33]

For planned surgery in Victoria, urgency categories target treatment within 30 days, 90 days or 12 months, but those measures do not capture the full pre-list outpatient journey.[^63][^64]

### Private route

Private care usually offers faster specialist choice and scheduling but may involve consultation, imaging, anaesthetic, hospital-excess and procedural gaps. A valid GP referral is needed for a Medicare specialist rebate; request written informed financial consent from every provider and check the insurer’s agreement/network arrangements.[^52][^65]

For a privately insured heart angiogram, the Australian Medical Costs Finder reports that 15% of patients had an out-of-pocket specialist cost in 2023–24 and notes typical hospital fees around $4,600 before insurance arrangements; figures are historical national guides, not quotes. For PCI with angiography, 24% had an out-of-pocket cost, with a typical $100 among those paying a gap, while typical hospital fees were around $15,000 before insurance coverage; Victorian specialist fees were typically $3,700 in those data.[^66][^67]

Private insurance does not usually cover ordinary outpatient consultations or all outpatient imaging gaps. Before proceeding, ask:

- Is this outpatient or admitted care?
- Which MBS items will be billed?
- What are the cardiologist, radiologist, interventionalist, assistant and anaesthetist fees?
- What are the hospital excess/co-payment and device costs?
- Is each doctor in the insurer’s no-gap/known-gap scheme?
- What is the likely cost if angiography proceeds immediately to PCI?

### Medicines and PBS

Generic statins and ezetimibe are widely accessible through community prescribing, subject to clinical indication and PBS rules. PCSK9-targeted therapies have strict authority criteria; current Australian sources describe eligibility for selected familial or symptomatic ASCVD patients whose LDL remains above threshold despite maximally tolerated statin and ezetimibe, with evolocumab/inclisiran available under specified restrictions.[^39][^68][^40]

An isolated asymptomatic CAC score may not satisfy “symptomatic ASCVD” PBS criteria. A specialist can clarify whether the documented anatomy and clinical history qualify, and whether a lower-cost oral combination is sufficient.

## Conflicting guidance and how to resolve it

The Australian evidence base contains an important tension. Older CSANZ-derived material recommended statin and aspirin treatment at higher CAC thresholds, whereas newer primary-prevention evidence is less supportive of aspirin in adults over 60 because bleeding offsets benefit. The durable consensus is strong for lipid lowering and risk-factor control; aspirin requires individual adjudication.[^46][^45][^43]

Another tension is whether very high CAC should trigger routine functional testing. Observational risk is undeniable, but randomised evidence that testing all asymptomatic high-CAC patients improves outcomes is lacking. Public services such as RMH therefore prioritise preventive management unless active symptoms or abnormal tests create a focused diagnostic question.[^33][^18]

International LDL targets are more explicit than Australia’s 2023 primary-prevention guideline. The best resolution is to document the intended risk category and agree a percentage LDL reduction plus an absolute level, rather than arguing over a threshold without defining whether this is primary prevention, chronic coronary disease or secondary prevention.[^69][^41][^42]

## Questions for the cardiologist

1. Was this a dedicated CAC scan, incidental calcium, or CTCA?
2. What are the exact total score, percentile and vessel-level distribution?
3. Is any left-main or proximal LAD disease described?
4. Was the lumen assessed, and were any CTCA segments non-diagnostic because of calcium?
5. Based on symptoms and pre-test likelihood, is CTCA, stress imaging or no further test most appropriate?
6. What result would make invasive angiography necessary?
7. What risk category are we treating: high-risk primary prevention, chronic coronary disease, or secondary prevention?
8. What LDL-C percentage reduction and absolute goal are appropriate, and when will it be rechecked?
9. If LDL remains above goal, when should ezetimibe or a PCSK9-targeted drug be added?
10. Is aspirin indicated despite age-related bleeding risk, and what is the explicit rationale?
11. What BP target is safe given standing BP, renal function and falls risk?
12. Is unrestricted exercise safe now, or should functional assessment precede vigorous activity?
13. Is echocardiography needed to assess LV function or valves?
14. Which symptoms should trigger 000 versus urgent clinic contact?
15. What are the public and private options, timeframes and full expected gaps?

## A 30-day action plan

### Today

- Treat active warning symptoms as an emergency and call 000.
- Obtain the complete August–September 2026 reports and image access details.
- List all prescribed and non-prescribed medicines, doses and adverse effects.
- Do not self-start or stop aspirin, statin, BP medication or supplements.

### Within days

- Book a long GP review for symptom assessment, ECG, BP and medication reconciliation.
- Complete lipid profile, HbA1c/glucose, renal function/eGFR, urine ACR, liver tests and other indicated baseline tests.
- Calculate Australian absolute risk for context, while explicitly recording imaging-proven plaque.
- Arrange an appropriately triaged cardiology referral if symptoms, extreme score, abnormal tests or management complexity justify it.

### Within weeks

- Start or intensify clinician-directed LDL and BP therapy.
- Select further testing only if it answers a defined question: anatomy, ischaemia, LV function or symptom cause.
- Establish smoking cessation, diet, activity and weight/sleep plans.
- Record treatment targets, monitoring dates and emergency instructions in writing.

### By 6–12 weeks

- Reassess symptoms, adherence, side effects, home BP and lipid response.
- Escalate lipid therapy if the agreed response is not achieved.
- Resolve any equivocal imaging or high-risk findings through cardiology review rather than repeating CAC.

## Evidence limits

Most CAC prognostic evidence is observational; it establishes strong risk prediction but does not prove that scanning itself improves outcomes. Thresholds vary among guidelines and populations, and Australian-specific event calibration remains less extensive than international cohort evidence.[^17][^18]

The individual prognosis cannot be calculated from “significant calcification” alone. Exact imaging, symptoms, competing illness, treatment exposure and patient preferences are indispensable. This report should be used to organise a consultation, not to choose prescriptions or procedures without one.

## Final clinical position

For a 67-year-old man, substantial CAC should be treated as a serious, actionable sign of coronary atherosclerosis—but not as proof of critical obstruction or an impending heart attack. The high-value response is to identify any unstable symptoms immediately, extract the exact imaging phenotype, intensify LDL/BP/smoking/diabetes management, and use CTCA, stress imaging or invasive angiography only when the clinical question warrants it.

In Melbourne, the fastest safe pathway is often a well-prepared GP review plus either a private cardiology appointment or a correctly triaged catchment-based public referral. Isolated asymptomatic CAC may not be accepted by some public tertiary clinics, making strong GP-led prevention—not repeated scanning—the central intervention.[^33]
